How much does a website cost in Perth in 2026?

A straight answer for Perth business owners: what websites really cost, what you get at each price, and the running costs most quotes leave out.

Tim DalyOctober 20265 min read

"How much does a website cost?" is the first question nearly every Perth business owner asks me. It's also the one most web designers dodge. You'll hear "it depends", then a discovery call, then a quote that arrives a week later with a number you weren't expecting.

It does depend. But not so much that you can't get a straight answer. Here's what websites actually cost in 2026, what you get at each level, and how to tell whether a quote is fair.

The short answer

For a professional small-business website in Australia, most owners pay somewhere between $2,000 and $6,000. Template builds from freelancers usually sit at the lower end of that, custom design and strategy at the upper end. Online shops and sites with bookings, payments or integrations start higher again.

Then there's the bottom of the market: the $299 and $499 offers you've probably seen in your Facebook feed. They're real, and I'll come back to them.

What you get at each price level

Under $1,000: the template special

A pre-made template with your logo and text dropped in. Often built offshore, usually sold through social ads. You'll get a few pages that look fine on a phone. You usually won't get local SEO, help with your words, or anyone who knows Perth. Hosting is often included for a month or two, then billed separately.

$1,500 to $3,000: the solid local site

This is where most tradies, cafes, clinics and service businesses land. Expect a site designed around your brand, written so customers understand what you do, set up for Google, and connected to your Google Business Profile. A good designer at this level will also show you how to make simple changes yourself.

$3,000 to $6,000: built to bring in work

More pages, custom design, booking or quote forms, proper copywriting and a plan for how local customers will find you. This is the level for businesses that rely on their website for a steady flow of enquiries.

$5,000 and up: shops and smart sites

Online shops, payment flows, CRM connections, AI chatbots and automation. The cost here is driven by what the site has to do, not how many pages it has.

What actually drives the price

When two quotes for "a 5-page website" are $800 and $4,000 apart, these are usually the reasons:

  • Design. A template adjusted to your colours is quicker than a design built around your brand and customers.
  • Words. Writing good copy is often half the job. Some quotes include it. Many quietly assume you'll supply it.
  • SEO setup. Page titles, local keywords, fast loading, Google Business Profile. Skipped on cheap builds, essential if you want to be found.
  • Features. Bookings, payments, quote forms and integrations each add time.
  • What happens after launch. Hosting, updates, backups and support. This is the cost most quotes leave out.

Don't forget the running costs

The build is only part of the cost. A website also needs hosting, a domain, security updates and backups. In Australia, website care plans that cover this typically run from about $50 to $200 a month, depending on how much support is included.

This is where many cheap offers make their money back. A $299 website with one month of hosting becomes a monthly bill you didn't plan for, often with no support attached. Always ask what the site will cost in year two, not just at launch.

So is a $299 website ever worth it?

Sometimes. If you just need a digital business card and you're happy to sort out hosting, updates and Google yourself, it can do the job. But if you want the website to bring in customers, you're usually paying twice: once for the cheap site, then again for the one that works. I wrote more about that in the real cost of a cheap website.

How to tell if a quote is fair

Before you sign, a good quote should clearly answer these:

  1. How many pages, and who writes the words?
  2. What's included for Google and local search?
  3. Who owns the site, the domain and the content?
  4. What will hosting and maintenance cost each month after launch?
  5. How many rounds of changes are included?

If you can't get straight answers, that tells you something too.
